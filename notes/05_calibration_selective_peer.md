## Q1. Definitions and formulas: calibration, ECE and its pitfalls, Brier score and decomposition, reliability diagrams, risk-coverage, AURC, selective accuracy, coverage at a target risk, conformal risk control (plus verification of the classic primaries)

### Takeaway
A confidence score is "calibrated" if, among cases given confidence p, a fraction p are correct; calibration (ECE, reliability diagrams, Brier) and ranking quality (AUROC, risk-coverage, AURC/AUGRC) are different properties, and an escalation policy needs the second more than the first (a monotone recalibration fixes calibration but never changes which items are escalated). Binned ECE is biased and unstable, so use equal-mass bins or CORP/PAV diagrams with uncertainty bands and report Brier plus its decomposition; for abstention, report the full risk-coverage curve, prefer AUGRC over AURC, and use a high-probability certificate (SGR/Learn-then-Test style) when you need a guaranteed error rate among answered items, because conformal risk control (CRC) guarantees an expectation, not the conditional error among accepted items.

Brier score and decomposition
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY for metadata, SECONDARY-ONLY for source] Brier, G. W., "Verification of forecasts expressed in terms of probability", Monthly Weather Review 78, 1–3 (1950). Record confirmed through Semantic Scholar; AMS page returned 403 — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1175/1520-0493%281950%29078%3C0001:VOFEIT%3E2.0.CO;2?fields=title,authors,year,venue,journal,publicationDate)
- [SECONDARY-ONLY] Binary Brier score BS = (1/N) Σ_t (f_t − o_t)², f_t = forecast probability of the event, o_t ∈ {0,1}; lower is better; always-0.5 forecaster scores 0.25; climatology scores UNC = ō(1−ō). (Formula from background knowledge; Brier's original 1950 multi-category form sums over classes.) The Brier skill score BSS = 1 − BS/BS_ref is used by KalshiBench (see Q2).
- [SECONDARY-ONLY for source] Murphy, A. H., "A new vector partition of the probability score", Journal of Applied Meteorology 12, 595–600 (1973), 1 Jun 1973 (record via Semantic Scholar; AMS page 403) — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1175/1520-0450%281973%29012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2?fields=title,authors,year,venue,journal,publicationDate)
- [SECONDARY-ONLY] Binned Murphy decomposition: BS = REL − RES + UNC, REL = (1/N) Σ_k n_k (f̄_k − ō_k)², RES = (1/N) Σ_k n_k (ō_k − ō)², UNC = ō(1−ō) (background knowledge; not read in Murphy's paper).
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] General form read in Dimitriadis et al.: S̄_X = MCB − DSC + UNC with MCB = S̄_X − S̄_C (miscalibration, C = recalibrated forecast), DSC = S̄_R − S̄_C (discrimination, R = reference/climatology), UNC = S̄_R; worked example (precipitation, Brier score) S̄_X = .266 = MCB .066 − DSC .044 + UNC .244. "Murphy's decomposition is exact in the discrete case, but fails to be exact under continuous forecasts" (so a binned REL/RES split has a residual unless forecasts are constant within bins) — [Dimitriadis et al. PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.03033)
- [SECONDARY-ONLY] Gneiting & Raftery, "Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation", JASA 102, 359–378 (2007) — standard reference for why Brier and log score are proper (record via Semantic Scholar) — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1198/016214506000001437?fields=title,authors,year,venue,journal,publicationDate)

Conformal risk control and other risk-control guarantees
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Angelopoulos, Bates, Fisch, Lei, Schuster, "Conformal Risk Control", ICLR 2024 (arXiv 4 Aug 2022, v4 13 Jun 2025). Procedure (Eq. 4): λ̂ = inf{ λ : (n/(n+1))·R̂_n(λ) + B/(n+1) ≤ α } with R̂_n the empirical mean loss on n calibration points, losses L_i(λ) non-increasing in λ, right-continuous, bounded by B, with L_i(λ_max) ≤ α a.s. - [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Angelopoulos, Bates, Candès, Jordan, Lei, "Learn then Test: Calibrating Predictive Algorithms to Achieve Risk Control" (arXiv 3 Oct 2021, rev. 29 Sep 2022): casts risk control as multiple hypothesis testing; finite-sample guarantees, no retraining — [arXiv:2110.01052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01052)

### Inferences
- A selective classifier's quantity of interest, R = E[ℓ·g]/E[g], is a ratio, so it is not itself a monotone bounded expected loss; CRC applied to loss ℓ·g controls the joint (generalized) risk E[ℓ·g] ≤ α, not the error rate among accepted items. A guarantee of the form "error among auto-resolved items ≤ r*" needs the SGR / Learn-then-Test / fixed-sequence-testing route (a binomial-tail certificate on the accepted subset), which is what Jung et al. use (Q3). This is my reading of the definitions above, not a statement made in any single paper.
- Because AURC weights low-coverage (high-confidence) failures heavily and AUGRC reports the joint failure rate, an oracle deployment that auto-resolves only a minority of items should report the full risk-coverage curve plus coverage at a pre-specified risk, not a single AURC.
- For binary YES/NO oracle questions, "top-label confidence" (confidence in whichever answer was chosen) and "probability of YES" are different quantities: ECE on P(YES) (KalshiBench setup) tests probability calibration of the event; ECE on top-label confidence (the oracle-paper setup, Q3) tests calibration of the decision. They are not interchangeable.
- With n in the low hundreds and a discrete confidence scale (Q4), a 10–15-bin ECE is mostly noise; the CORP diagram with bands, or Brier with bootstrap intervals, is more defensible.

## Q2. LLM confidence: verbalized vs logit-based vs probe-based vs consistency-based (Kadavath, Tian, Xiong, Kuhn, Farquhar, forecasting probes, KalshiBench)

### Takeaway
Pre-trained models' token probabilities are well calibrated on multiple-choice formats but RLHF damages that; verbalized confidence from RLHF models is usually better calibrated than their logits yet clusters at a few high values, is overconfident on hard tasks, and has weak discrimination (average AUROC about 0.51–0.63 for vanilla prompts in Xiong et al.); consistency/semantic-entropy and trained probes give better discrimination, and in forecasting a probe matched verbalized AUROC while cutting ECE several-fold. Evidence that extended reasoning improves calibration (Yoon et al.) conflicts with KalshiBench, where the reasoning-heavy model was worst calibrated.

### Cited Findings

Logit/P(True)/P(IK) (Kadavath et al.)
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Kadavath et al., "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know", arXiv 11 Jul 2022 (v4 21 Nov 2022), 36 authors (first author Saurav Kadavath, last Jared Kaplan): larger models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false formats; P(True) (the model's probability that its own sample is correct) and P(IK) ("I know", a value head trained to predict whether the model can answer) are introduced; "models perform well at predicting P(IK) and partially generalize across tasks" but calibration on new tasks is hard — [arXiv:2207.05221](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)

### Inferences
- Ranking of confidence sources by discrimination (AUROC) is not the same as ranking by calibration: in the forecasting probe paper the probe and verbalized AUROC are essentially equal (0.756 vs 0.758) while ECE differs by 2–5×; a monotone recalibration of verbalized confidence would close most of the ECE gap without changing which items are escalated.
- Yoon et al. (reasoning helps calibration on 33 of 36 settings) and KalshiBench (reasoning-heavy GPT-5.2-XHigh worst, ECE 0.395) can both be right: the tasks (benchmarks with checkable answers vs future-event forecasting), elicitation prompts and sample sizes differ, and KalshiBench uses one prompt on 300 questions. The conflict is not resolved by either paper.
- For a decision system, the cheapest robust options in the evidence above are (a) consistency across independent samples or models and (b) a small supervised calibrator or probe trained on labeled outcomes (Kapoor: about 1,000 labels); raw verbalized confidence should be treated as an ordinal feature, not a probability.

## Q4. Abstention under insufficient evidence in RAG; verbalized confidence for RAG / fact verification; prompt sensitivity, round numbers, and the "absence of evidence -> answer NO" bias

### Takeaway
Direct evidence is thin: no paper found reports AUROC of verbalized confidence specifically for retrieval-augmented fact verification. What is established: (1) strong LLMs answer rather than abstain when retrieved context is insufficient (Sufficient Context: Gemini 1.5 Pro hallucinated on 40% and GPT-4o 29% of insufficient-context Musique questions); (2) retrieval can increase hallucination by reducing abstention (CRAG); (3) verbalized confidence is coarse (more than 78% of answers on three values on a 0–100 scale) and prompt-dependent; (4) a context-sufficiency signal plus confidence improves selective accuracy by 2–10 points; and (5) the oracle-resolution paper documents a retrieval miss turning into unanimous NO, but no study quantifies a general "NO default".

### Inferences
- With CRAG-style scoring (+1 correct, 0 abstain, −1 wrong) the break-even confidence for answering is 0.5 (expected score 2p − 1 > 0); with Chow's cost d the threshold is 1 − d. Whether an oracle should abstain therefore depends on the stated cost of a wrong resolution versus an escalation, which the oracle paper does not specify.
- A coarse verbalized scale makes any confidence threshold behave as a step function (many ties at 90/95/100), so a median-threshold rule like Kota's ≥ 0.91 only works because three confidences are averaged; a single-model verbalized threshold would split ties arbitrarily and make coverage-at-risk curves lumpy.
- Retrieval misses are structurally asymmetric for event-resolution questions: a missing mention supports "no" only under a closed-world reading. The documented case (Kota 4.4.1) and the FEVER-style Not-Enough-Evidence over-assignment (Bakke & Heggelund) suggest forcing a binary verdict with no insufficiency option converts evidence gaps into confident NO answers; a three-way output (YES / NO / INSUFFICIENT) scored with an explicit abstention utility (CRAG or I-CALM-style) is the evidence-backed design, but I found no study that measures this specific effect.
- Sufficient Context suggests a cheap, separable abstention feature for oracle pipelines: an autorater of "does the retrieved evidence suffice to decide?" (93% accuracy in that paper) combined with ensemble agreement.

## Q5. Peer prediction: classic results and recent applications to LLMs

### Takeaway
Classic peer prediction pays agents for agreement with peers so truthful reporting is an equilibrium without ground truth, but every classic scheme also has uninformative (collusive or low-effort) equilibria; multi-task mechanisms (Dasgupta-Ghosh, Correlated Agreement, DMI) strengthen this, and Gao et al. showed in experiments and theory that peer prediction can be worse than spot-checking against a small amount of ground truth. The two LLM papers adapt the machinery: Chen et al. (NeurIPS 2025) use a determinant mutual-information score among a generator and discriminators; Qiu, Carroll and Allen (ICLR 2026) use a log-score "mutual predictability" reward and report that its resistance to deception grows with the capability gap while LLM-as-a-judge degrades.

### Cited Findings

Classic mechanisms
- [SECONDARY-ONLY for source] Miller, Resnick, Zeckhauser, "Eliciting Informative Feedback: The Peer-Prediction Method", Management Science 51, 1359–1373 (2005), DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1050.0379 (Semantic Scholar record; INFORMS page 403; issue number not in the record) — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1287/mnsc.1050.0379?fields=title,authors,year,venue,journal,externalIds), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1050.0379)
- [SECONDARY-ONLY for source] Prelec, "A Bayesian Truth Serum for Subjective Data", Science 306, 462–466, 15 Oct 2004 (record via Semantic Scholar; Science page 403, Crossref 429) — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1126/science.1102081?fields=title,authors,year,venue,journal,publicationDate), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1102081)

- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Radanovic & Faltings, "A Robust Bayesian Truth Serum for Non-Binary Signals", AAAI 2013 (Vol. 27 No. 1, pp. 833–839, 30 Jun 2013, DOI 10.1609/aaai.v27i1.8677): RBTS operates without the mechanism knowing a common prior; the multi-valued RBTS "generalizes RBTS to operate on both small populations and non-binary signals" — [AAAI OJS](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/8677)
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Dasgupta & Ghosh, "Crowdsourced Judgement Elicitation with Endogenous Proficiency", WWW 2013 (arXiv 4 Mar 2013): multi-task mechanism that "penalizes for blind agreement by subtracting out a statistic term"; maximum effort plus truthful reporting is a Nash equilibrium with the highest payoff for all participants, even with heterogeneous abilities and mixed strategies; needs little prior information and only agents' own reports — [arXiv:1303.0799](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.0799)
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Shnayder, Agarwal, Frongillo, Parkes, "Informed Truthfulness in Multi-Task Peer Prediction" (arXiv 1603.03151; extended version of the EC 2016 paper, per the PDF): characterises signal distributions for which the Dasgupta–Ghosh mechanism stays strongly truthful with non-binary signals; introduces Correlated Agreement (CA) with "informed truthfulness" (no profile pays more in equilibrium than truthful reporting, truthful strictly beats uninformed strategies); "maximally strongly truthful" in a broad class; works with as few as two agents and three tasks; the detail-free version needs O(n³ log(1/δ)/ε²) tasks for ε-informed truthfulness with n signals; the designer needs the correlation structure of signals; the "categorical" condition holds for only about one third of the three-signal models fitted to MOOC data (as extracted) — [arXiv:1603.03151](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.03151), [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.03151)

- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Gao, Mao, Chen, Adams, "Trick or Treat: Putting Peer Prediction to the Test", EC 2014: in an online experiment "participants successfully coordinate on uninformative equilibria and the truthful equilibrium is not focal"; "most players are consistently truthful in the absence of peer prediction" — [Princeton LIPS entry](https://lips.cs.princeton.edu/bibliography/gao2014peer/)
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Gao, Wright, Leyton-Brown, "Incentivizing Evaluation via Limited Access to Ground Truth: Peer-Prediction Makes Things Worse" (arXiv cs.GT, 22 Jun 2016): peer-prediction mechanisms admit equilibria in which agents avoid the cost of careful evaluation and coordinate on cheap signals (unavoidable when agents can coordinate on low-cost signals); a spot-check mechanism (compare with a trusted evaluator with probability p, constant payment otherwise) gives dominant-strategy truthfulness with a lower probability of querying ground truth than peer prediction needs; theoretical paper. Applications to LLMs
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Chen, Zhu, Han, Li, Li, Dai, "Incentivizing Truthful Language Models via Peer Elicitation Games", NeurIPS 2025 poster (arXiv 2505.13636): Peer Elicitation Games (PEG), a "training-free, game-theoretic framework" with a generator and multiple discriminators from distinct base models; "a determinant-based mutual information score that provably incentivizes truthful reporting without requiring ground-truth labels"; discriminators achieve sublinear regret (O(√T)) and last-iterate convergence to a truthful Nash equilibrium; reported: "more than a 10% improvement in accuracy on the most challenging benchmarks — ARC-Challenge and MMLU", "Smaller models (e.g., 7B parameters) using PEG can match or outperform much larger models (e.g., 65B)", nearly 70% on MMLU and 86% on ARC-Challenge (numbers as extracted from the PDF; the poster abstract only says "enhanced factual accuracy") — [arXiv:2505.13636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13636), [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13636), [NeurIPS 2025 poster](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/119902)
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Qiu, Carroll, Allen, "Truthfulness Despite Weak Supervision: Evaluating and Training LLMs Using Peer Prediction" (arXiv 28 Jan 2026), ICLR 2026 poster (Thursday 23 Apr 2026): rewards "honest and informative answers over deceptive and uninformative ones" using "a metric based on mutual predictability and without requiring ground truth labels"; abstract claims: an 8B model trained with peer-prediction rewards "recovers most of the drop in truthfulness due to prior malicious finetuning, even when the reward is produced by a 0.135B language model with no finetuning"; "inverse scaling property": resistance to deception strengthens as the capability gap widens; "LLM-as-a-Judge become worse than random guess when facing deceptive models 5-20× the judge's size, while peer prediction thrives when such gaps are large, including in cases with over 100× size difference"; models up to 405B parameters — [arXiv:2601.20299](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20299), [ICLR 2026 page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10007556)
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Qiu et al. method and setup (as extracted from the PDF): Algorithm 1 gives a source participant the reward log Pr_j(A_t | A_s) − log Pr_j(A_t) (how much the source's answer helps an expert model j predict a target participant's answer); Theorem 1: with participant scores S^A_i/(nm) and expert scores S^J_j/n², honest answering and honest expert reporting form a Bayesian Nash equilibrium with maximum ex-ante payoff; Theorem 2 handles disagreement in priors drawn i.i.d. from a common distribution D with bounded variability. Stated limitation: theorems cover unilateral deception, not collusion among participants; peer prediction works better on STEM than non-STEM domains (humanities ρ = 0.0178 per Appendix A.3) — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.20299)

### Inferences
- The "inverse scaling" label refers to the detector's reliability improving as the target model gets larger than the judge; it is not a statement that bigger models are less truthful. The extracted setup lists judges up to 7B and participants up to 27B, while the abstract says models up to 405B; the claim "5–20×" is relative to the judge's size and the "over 100×" case matches a 0.135B expert against a 27B participant (about 200×). The 405B experiments were not located in the extraction — check the PDF.
- PEG's "determinant-based mutual information" is Kong's DMI score and Qiu et al.'s score is a pointwise-mutual-information (log-score) variant, so both recent LLM papers sit inside the Kong–Schoenebeck mutual-information family, with the same standing caveats: informative-peer and conditional-independence assumptions, no protection from collusion (Qiu et al. state this), and, per Gao et al., the possibility of coordinated low-effort equilibria. For LLM "agents" the incentive framing is best read as a training/evaluation signal rather than a claim about strategic behaviour.
- Relevance to oracle design: peer-prediction scores would give a ground-truth-free signal about which resolver or model is informative, but all guarantees are equilibrium statements; none of the verified papers provides a finite-sample error-rate guarantee on the resolved outcome, which is what a risk-controlled cascade (Q3) provides.

## Q6. Statistical practice for comparing two classifiers / abstention policies on the same items (McNemar, paired bootstrap, cluster bootstrap, equivalence testing / TOST, Holm) with standard references

### Takeaway
For two systems scored on the same items, use paired methods: McNemar (preferably mid-p or asymptotic rather than the conservative exact conditional test) on the discordant pairs, a paired bootstrap for any metric that is not a simple proportion, a cluster bootstrap or clustered standard errors when items are not independent, TOST when the claim is "no worse than", and Holm for multiple comparisons. For selective prediction the pairing is less trivial because the two policies accept different item subsets, so compare utilities or risk at matched coverage with the threshold re-selected inside each bootstrap resample.

### Inferences
Recommended practice assembled from the references above (my synthesis, not a quotation from any one source):
1. Fixed operating point, same item set: a paired comparison of per-item correctness; exact or mid-p McNemar on the discordant counts (b, c), with the Newcombe paired-difference interval for the effect size; report both, since Fagerland et al. favour mid-p/asymptotic over the exact conditional test.
2. Abstention policies: the accepted sets differ, so the discordance table is not the right object. Compare (a) a per-item utility (for example +1 correct, 0 abstain/escalate, −c wrong) on the full item set with a paired bootstrap or clustered SE on the per-item differences, and (b) selective risk at matched coverage. In (b) resample items, re-select thresholds inside each resample, and recompute risk at the target coverage, so threshold-selection variance is included; report the full risk-coverage curves with bootstrap bands and AUGRC differences (Traub et al.).
3. Dependent items (same event, same entity, same time window, repeated runs of a stochastic model): resample whole clusters (cluster bootstrap, Field & Welsh), or use clustered SEs (Miller); use the wild cluster bootstrap-t when there are few clusters (Cameron et al.).
4. Small N (a few hundred items or fewer, as in KalshiBench N = 300 or Kota N = 1,189): use Wilson/Beta-Bernoulli intervals, not CLT intervals (Bowyer et al.); do a power analysis before data collection (Miller).
5. Claims of "no worse than" (an automated policy versus human arbitration, a cheaper cascade versus the full model): pre-register an equivalence or non-inferiority margin from the smallest cost-relevant difference and run TOST (Schuirmann; Lakens); a non-significant McNemar result is not evidence of equivalence.
6. Many policies, thresholds, or datasets: Holm step-down for family-wise error across the family of tests; for several classifiers over many datasets use Demšar's Friedman/Nemenyi procedure.
7. Model-error correlation inflates apparent independence: in Kota the pairwise error correlation is 0.529–0.689 and when DeepSeek is wrong Llama is also wrong 77.0% of the time, so ensemble gains and agreement-based confidence rest on far fewer effectively independent errors than the number of models suggests.

### Gaps
- Holm 1979, Efron 1979 and Efron–Tibshirani 1993 could not be opened; Dietterich 1998 and Fagerland et al. 2013 were confirmed only as records (the Fagerland title states its conclusion).
- No paper was found that specifically treats paired inference for selective-prediction policies with different accepted sets; recommendation 2 is my synthesis.
- Card et al. (EMNLP 2020, power in NLP), Koehn (2004, paired bootstrap) and Wellek's equivalence-testing monograph were not opened and are not cited.
