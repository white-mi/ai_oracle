## 1. Multi-agent debate (MAD) versus simple voting / ensembling

### Takeaway
The best-controlled evidence says most of what MAD delivers is the majority-vote effect of sampling several answers, not the debate itself: Choi et al. (NeurIPS 2025) show voting beats debate on 7 benchmarks and prove debate is a martingale; Smit et al., Wang et al. and Zhang et al. find MAD fails to reliably beat self-consistency or well-prompted single agents. The original Du et al. result is real but much smaller than the "70% to 95%" figure attributed to it in a 2026 oracle paper: arithmetic went from 67.0% to 81.8%, with ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo-0301), 3 agents, 2 rounds.

### Inferences
- Evidence is two-sided only at the margins: Du et al. (strong, 2023 ChatGPT, 100 problems, N = 3) show debate > majority (81.8 vs 69.0 on arithmetic); Choi et al. (7–8B models, N = 5, 7 benchmarks) show majority > debate. Plausible moderators: model strength, task verifiability (arithmetic has checkable derivations), and whether round-1 answers are diverse. This is my reading; no paper tests it directly.
- A defensible default for aggregation design: independent round-1 answers plus a calibrated vote or confidence-weighted vote; use debate only as an escalation step for split votes (Kota: splits are 57.8% accurate vs 88.3% for unanimous), and never treat post-debate consensus as extra evidence (Kota: converge-after-round-2 cases are only 56.9% accurate).
- ICE-style gains reported against the mean individual model overstate the benefit of deliberation, because a plain majority vote of the same round-1 answers is not reported.

## 2. Correlated errors across LLMs and what it implies for ensembles

### Takeaway
LLM errors are strongly correlated and the correlation grows with capability: Kim et al. find about 60% agreement on wrong answers (HELM, 3 wrong options so chance ≈33%) across 350+ models, Goel et al. find similarity rises with capability, and 2026 work finds 9 LLM judges carry about 2 independent votes. For equicorrelated voters the effective number of independent votes is capped near 1/ρ and majority accuracy plateaus well below 1, which matches observed oracle results (Kota: three ~82% models give 82.9%, not the ~91% independence predicts).

### Cited Findings
**Empirical correlation**
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Kim, Garg, Peng, Garg, "Correlated Errors in Large Language Models", ICML 2025, PMLR 267:30038–30066; arXiv 2506.07962 (9 Jun 2025). 349 HuggingFace models on 12,032 MMLU MCQs and 71 HELM models on 14,042 MCQs; models "agree on average about 60% of the time when both models are incorrect" on HELM (chance 1/3) and 42.3% on HuggingFace (chance 12.7%). Shared architecture and provider raise correlation, but "larger and more accurate models have highly correlated errors, even with distinct architectures and providers." LLM-as-judge: each judge inflates the accuracy of less accurate models, especially same-provider ones. Hiring simulation (60 applicants, 30 firms, 20 LLMs): about 20% of applicants stay systematically excluded under random LLM selection; applicants are about 7× more likely to match with 30 applications than with 1 under polyculture but only 2× under monoculture. Code and data: https://github.com/nikhgarg/llm_correlated_errors_public. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/kim25e.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962); [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.07962)

- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Song, "Information Homogenization Induces Herding in Retrieval-Augmented LLM Agent Markets", ICLR 2026 workshop (Financial AI), OpenReview 8v4FskZp8I. **Condorcet jury theorem with correlated voters; effective sample size**
- [SECONDARY-ONLY] Kaniovski's working paper "An Invariance Result for Homogeneous Juries with Correlated Votes" (WIFO, 2008) summarizes the classical line: Ladha (1992) numerical example, Berg (1993) urn-process models, Boland (1989) opinion leaders, Bahadur (1961) representation; it notes these works generally conclude that the jury theorem survives only under low correlation, and proves invariance of the majority probability to second-order correlations under stated conditions. I did not open Ladha or Berg themselves; their bibliographic details (e.g., Ladha, AJPS 1992) are from memory and unverified. — [Kaniovski PDF](https://Serguei.Kaniovski.wifo.ac.at/fileadmin/pdf/condorcet_inv_corr.pdf)
- [SECONDARY-ONLY] Kish design effect for equal-size clusters with intraclass correlation ρ: Deff = 1 + (n* − 1)ρ, effective sample size n_eff = n / Deff (Kish 1965). — [Wikipedia: Design effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_effect)
- [SECONDARY-ONLY (alphaxiv mirror)] Sasahara, Naito, Fujie, "A latent dimension of Condorcet's jury theorem for multiple AI advisers", arXiv 2609.14438 (13 Sep 2026): even independent, competent advisers show visible disagreement that approaches certainty faster than collective reliability improves when individual accuracy is below 0.8, so a correct panel can look fractured. — [alphaxiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.14438)
- [DERIVED] For exchangeable binary votes with mean competence p and pairwise correlation ρ: Var(mean vote) = p(1−p)[1+(n−1)ρ]/n, so n_eff = n/(1+(n−1)ρ) → 1/ρ as n → ∞. By de Finetti the votes are a Beta(ps, (1−p)s) mixture with s = 1/ρ − 1, so majority accuracy converges to P(Π > ½), not to 1 (computed with scipy beta-binomial; script `ess.py` in the session scratchpad). Selected values: p = 0.70, ρ = 0: n = 11 → 0.922, n = 101 → 1.000; ρ = 0.1: n = 3 → 0.762, n = 11 → 0.837, n = 101 → 0.891, limit 0.900; ρ = 0.3: limit 0.773; ρ = 0.5: limit 0.728. p = 0.82: ρ = 0 → n = 3 gives 0.9145; ρ = 0.3 → limit 0.897; ρ = 0.6 → n = 3 gives 0.8294, limit 0.8375. Kohli's "9 judges ≈ 2 votes" implies average ρ = (9/2 − 1)/8 ≈ 0.44; Kota's pairwise error correlations 0.53–0.69 give n_eff ≈ 1.26–1.46 for n = 3.

### Inferences
- [DERIVED] With ρ ≈ 0.6 and p ≈ 0.82, an exchangeable model predicts a 3-model majority accuracy of 0.829, essentially Kota's observed 82.93%. This is a coincidence-level check (the three models have unequal accuracies and the exchangeable model is an approximation), but it suggests Kish/beta-binomial is a usable first-order model for LLM panels.
- Error correlation rises with capability (Kim, Goel) and with shared retrieval (Song, Kota), so adding agents from the same frontier pool gives sharply diminishing returns; the number that matters is n_eff ≈ 1/ρ, not n.
- The Kish/equicorrelation formula assumes one common ρ and exchangeability; in LLM panels correlation is item-dependent (hard items fail for all). That is what the beta mixture captures, but a calibrated ρ must be estimated on the target task, and ρ measured on benchmarks need not transfer to evidence-interpretation tasks.

### Gaps
- No study found that holds the model fixed and varies retrieval (shared vs independent) while measuring answer-error correlation on a fact-checking task; Song (actions) and Kota (anecdotal retrieval failures) are the closest.
- Ladha (1992), Berg (1993), Boland (1989) were not read in primary form; no verified theorem statement with explicit conditions under correlation beyond the Kaniovski summary and the derivation above.
- Kohli, Shu, Kim (D.), Aitchison, Douven, Sasahara have no public code or data link in what I read; two of these are alphaxiv-only reads.

## 3. "Swarm" and many-agent methods

### Takeaway
Three different things are called "swarm": (a) sampling and voting many copies of one model (Agent Forest, self-consistency), which works but is capped by the correlation above; (b) layered multi-model synthesis (Mixture-of-Agents); (c) particle-swarm optimization over model weights or role graphs (Model Swarms, Heterogeneous Swarms), which is model adaptation, not answer aggregation. For the prediction-market and "social simulation" swarms (PolySwarm, MiroFish/OASIS) I found no independent accuracy evidence: PolySwarm's quantitative results could not be confirmed, MiroFish's README reports no benchmarks, and the one independent test is a single-day anecdote in which the swarm matched a single call.

## 4. Debate and voting on evidence-interpretation tasks with shared documents: conformity, minority-correct flips, round-1 prompt sensitivity

### Takeaway
Only a handful of papers touch shared-document verification, and none runs a controlled conformity study there. The closest is Kota's oracle-resolution test (identical evidence packets): deliberation lowered accuracy 4–8 pp and flipped as many correct answers to wrong as the reverse. General (document-free) conformity work shows large, reproducible peer-pressure effects: a unanimous wrong majority reverses 22.8% to 71.0% of correct answers depending on the dataset.

- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Indirect evidence only (see Gaps for the absence of a direct study): persona perturbations alter reasoning trajectories and answers (CHOIR, §3); question reformulation is a stronger diversity source than model swaps in binary QA (Rosales & Miret, §3); Liang et al. show the judge favors same-backbone debaters; Choi et al.'s martingale result makes the round-1 belief distribution the quantity that determines the expected final accuracy. — [CHOIR](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.2175/); [Rosales & Miret](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.400/); [Choi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2)

### Inferences
- Conformity is strongest for small open models in the studies above (7–32B); the only frontier-ish oracle test (Kota) shows a smaller, symmetric flip rate (2.47% of revisions, 45 vs 43) but still a net accuracy loss at the question level (76.1% vs 82.9%), because converged-after-debate answers are poor (56.9%). Flip counts alone understate the harm.
- Round-1 independence is the quantity to protect: anonymized peer reasoning, All-Agents Drafting (Kaesberg) and dissent-preserving designs (a single correct dissenter cuts the failure yield by 54–73 pp per Kumarappan & Mujoo) all aim at that.
- Shared documents create a common-mode failure that no amount of debate can fix (Kota's 164 "hard core" questions, 14.0%, wrong under both architectures); evidence diversity (different retrieval paths) is the untested remedy.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of conformity, minority-correct flips, or round-1 prompt sensitivity on FEVER-style or legal tasks with a fixed shared document set; DebateCV's full text could not be read.
- Kasprova, Yao, Wynn, Hussain, Kumarappan use small or open models and non-document tasks; their effect sizes may not transfer to frontier models reading evidence.

## 5. Theory to reuse: martingales, DeGroot, cascades, mean-field models of LLM populations

### Takeaway
Three classical frames fit debate dynamics: (i) Bayesian posteriors are martingales (Choi et al. prove it for LLM debate, so debate alone cannot raise expected correctness); (ii) DeGroot/Friedkin–Johnsen averaging, which Golub and Jackson show leads to truth iff no agent's influence stays non-vanishing; (iii) sequential-observation herding/information cascades (Banerjee; Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch), which Jain and Krishnamurthy prove for sequential Bayesian LLM agents. Fitted LLM data (2026) reject plain DeGroot: internal bias dominates.

### Cited Findings
**Martingale / Bayesian updating**
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Choi et al. Theorem 2: under a Dirichlet-Compound-Multinomial belief model, E[p_{i,t} | α_{t−1}] = p_{i,t−1}, i.e., debate is a martingale in each agent's belief on the correct answer, so "debate alone does not improve expected correctness"; Theorem 1 gives the voting amplification bound. — [Choi et al. HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2)
- [SECONDARY-ONLY] General fact: a Doob martingale (E[X | F_t]) is the "sequentially updated best estimate of X as more information becomes available". (The Wikipedia page does not name Bayesian updating or state the tower property explicitly.) — [Wikipedia: Martingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martingale_(probability_theory))
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Estornell & Liu: debate as Bayesian inference over latent concepts; with similar models it converges to the majority opinion, and expected correctness falls as more models share a misconception (Thm 5.1–5.4). — [paper PDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/32e07a110c6c6acf1afbf2bf82b614ad-Paper-Conference.pdf)

**DeGroot and learning in networks**
- [SECONDARY-ONLY] DeGroot (1974), "Reaching a Consensus", JASA 69(345):118–121: x(t+1) = W x(t) with a row-stochastic trust matrix W; beliefs converge for any initial beliefs iff every strongly connected closed set of nodes is aperiodic. — [Wikipedia: DeGroot learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeGroot_learning)
- [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] Golub & Jackson, "Naïve Learning in Social Networks and the Wisdom of Crowds", American Economic Journal: Microeconomics 2(1):112–149 (Feb 2010), DOI 10.1257/mic.2.1.112: "all opinions in a large society converge to the truth if and only if the influence of the most influential agent vanishes as the society grows". — [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mic.2.1.112)

### Inferences
- A usable minimal model for oracle aggregation: each agent holds a posterior over the correct answer; one round of peer exchange is a DeGroot-type averaging with an internal-bias term (Probine et al.) plus anchoring (Friedkin–Johnsen). Under Choi's martingale result the expected mean belief is unchanged by averaging; the realized variance shrinks, so debate converts disagreement into consensus (which voting could count as a signal) without adding information, and a biased fixed point (shared misconception) is exactly Estornell & Liu's failure mode.
- Golub–Jackson's condition (no vanishing-influence failure) maps to "no single agent or evidence packet dominates"; a shared retrieval pack is effectively one super-influential node, so it keeps n_eff near 1 regardless of agent count. This is my mapping, not a result from the papers.
- Mean-field treatments of LLM agents so far fit macroscopic rates (Naming Game π, φ; MF-LLM IB-Tune) rather than deriving a mean-field game from agent objectives; there is room for a Bayesian-agent mean-field limit with common-signal (shared document) terms, which would play the role of common noise in MFG theory. Speculative.

### Gaps
- DeGroot, Friedkin–Johnsen, Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch and Ladha were not read in primary form (Wikipedia or secondary summaries only); no verified formal statement of the BHW cascade condition (e.g., the two-excess-signals rule) from the original.
- The assumptions B1–B3 of Jain & Krishnamurthy's Theorem 2 were not extracted.
- No paper found that proves a mean-field or voter-model limit for LLM debate with shared evidence; the Naming Game and MF-LLM papers are the nearest.
